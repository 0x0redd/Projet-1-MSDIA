- **ABSTRACT** Brain tumors rank among the most lethal forms of cancer, and their early detection is crucial for improving patient outcomes. Beyond timely identification, accurately determining tumor type and grade is essential for guiding treatment strategies. While Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a mainstay in tumor detection and diagnosis, the precise classification of brain tumors remains challenging due to variations in their morphology, including shape and size. In recent years, artificial intelligence—particularly machine learning methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and k-nearest neighbors (KNN), and deep learning architectures including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)—has emerged as a powerful tool for classifying brain tumor types and grades from MR images. This review provides a comprehensive examination of contemporary studies that leverage these methods for tumor classification. The analysis is based on a structured search of major scientific databases and includes 81 studies published between 2014 and 2025 that met predefined inclusion criteria. Specifically, it analyzes the classification techniques employed, the datasets and their characteristics, feature extraction approaches, and performance evaluations reported in the literature. Reported results show that deep learning methods generally outperform traditional machine learning models, with many studies achieving accuracies above 90% in tumor type or grade classification tasks. In addition to summarizing existing work, this review provides a unified perspective by jointly examining tumor type and multi-class grade classification studies, highlighting understudied areas such as non-glioma grade prediction and common dataset limitations. By synthesizing existing findings, the review identifies key research gaps and proposes directions for future investigations. A key future direction highlighted in this review is the need for more comprehensive multi-class tumor grade prediction studies, particularly for non-glioma tumor types. The insights presented here aim to aid researchers and clinicians in developing more accurate and efficient brain tumor classification systems, leading to earlier diagnosis, reduced diagnostic variability, and more informed treatment planning in clinical practice.

## _C. BRAIN IMAGING TECHNIQUES_ 

Computed tomography (CT), MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), and blood and urine tests are used to diagnose brain tumors. In cases of brain tumors, imaging techniques are often preferred instead of biopsies, as the biopsy process can be time-consuming when confirming the tumor’s type and grade. The most commonly used imaging methods are MRI, PET, and CT [9], [10]. 

PET scans are nuclear imaging techniques. By injecting a radioactive tracer, usually fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), the targeted organ can be imaged in three dimensions. The tracers attach to compounds such as glucose. Since brain and cancer cells retain glucose, the affected area can be observed more clearly during the scan. However, high-grade tumors cannot always be distinguished on FDG-PET images [11]. 

CT scans provide detailed analyses. Images are obtained using X-rays, which emit high levels of radiation. CT scans are generally not recommended for intensive examinations, as repeated scans significantly increase radiation exposure. In such cases, MRI is usually preferred [11], [12]. 

MRI is a diagnostic modality that utilizes potent magnetic fields and radiofrequency signals to produce high-resolution 

pictures of inside tissues and organs. MRI is particularly effective for detecting brain malignancies. Brain tumors may exhibit various characteristics, including iso-intensity (where tumor tissues show similar signals to surrounding tissue), hypointensity (where regions appear darker than tumor tissue), hyperintensity (regions with increased signal intensity), tumor heterogeneity, and tumor homogeneity [13]. Ambiguities in the imaging process may also arise due to conditions such as perilesional edema in neighboring tissues. In comparison to CT and PET, MRI provides enhanced soft-tissue contrast, avoids ionizing radiation exposure, and delivers various sequences that yield complementing structural and pathological insights. These advantages make MRI the preferred imaging modality for AI-based brain tumor classification, as it enables more accurate delineation of tumor boundaries, edema, and infiltration patterns. Consequently, most machine learning and deep learning studies in the literature rely primarily on MRI rather than CT or PET. 

Due to these complexities, classifying or grading brain tumors can be challenging for medical experts [14]. Many researchers are working on developing faster and easier diagnostic methods based on MR images. MR imaging enables visualization of the brain in three distinct planes, adding depth to the images. ## _D. BRAIN TUMOR DATASETS_ 

It has emerged as a result of the studies we have reviewed that the data required to conduct studies on brain tumors are generally obtained from open-source sources. In the literature, the data most preferred by researchers are actually BraTS, Figshare and Cancer Imaging Archive. The case numbers in these data sets are given in Table 1. All of these datasets are openly accessible for research purposes. 

BraTS (Brain Tumor Segmentation) organizes an annual competition focused on brain tumor segmentation and dataset usage [17]. This competition allows researchers to test and compare their algorithms. The BraTS dataset focuses on a single type of tumor, glioma. It was first introduced in 2012 with 50 cases and has increased its sample size every year. The latest version, released in 2023, contains 2001 cases. Both high-grade and low-grade glioma cases are included, with detailed descriptions provided in Table 1. 

The Figshare brain tumor dataset comprises 3,064 T1weighted contrast-enhanced images depicting three distinct types of brain tumors [18]. It includes 233 cases, consisting of meningioma (708 slices), glioma (1,426 slices), and pituitary tumor (930 slices). 

The TCGA-GBM dataset, part of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), focuses on Glioblastoma Multiforme (GBM) [19]. It contains clinical, genetic, pathological, and radiological data. GBM radiological images with 262 cases are stored in the Cancer Imaging Archive.

## _A. IMAGE PRE-PROCESSING AND FEATURE EXTRACTION TECHNIQUES_ 

In image data, certain preprocessing steps are undertaken to make raw images suitable for analysis and processing. MRI images can be acquired from different devices, or the technology used may change over time. Such variations alter the intensity values of MRI images. Since these differences can reduce the performance of AI methods, normalization techniques are often employed. These techniques allow the image data to produce more accurate and consistent results. Normalization techniques can be of various types, such as min-max normalization, Z-score normalization, intensity normalization, etc. Models work with images of specific

Another pre-processing method involves the removal of non-brain regions like the skull, eyes, and nose from the brain MRI images, which is due to the nature of these images. This procedure, known as skull stripping in the literature, is performed because these regions are unnecessary for tumor classification and may reduce classification accuracy. In brain MRI images, the image registration process is also used. This process involves transforming the images into a single coordinate system. To provide more accurate results about the tumor in patients, multiple MRI images of the patient are examined. Since these images are taken at different times and in different modalities, it is necessary to integrate the data. MRI images may contain noise due to changes in the magnetic field [21]. Filtering methods are used to eliminate noise, which can be achieved with methods like Gaussian filtering and mean filtering. Subsequently, enhancing the contrast can provide a clearer image. This ensures successful results during feature extraction and the classification phase. Feature extraction is an important step in making sense of raw data and making it usable. Raw images are difficult to analyse directly. Thanks to feature extraction, the model avoids unnecessary details and can focus on features that will be effective in classification. In brain tumors, feature extraction is often performed after the segmentation stage. Tissue features are generally used for distinguishing brains with tumors. Texture features can be derived by the application of statistical and spectral methodologies. In MRI-based brain tumor analysis, feature extraction techniques are widely used to capture tissue heterogeneity, texture characteristics, and structural patterns that are discriminative for tumor classification. The Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) is frequently employed because it decomposes images into multi-resolution subbands, enabling the representation of both coarse and fine-scale texture variations that are often associated with heterogeneous tumors such as gliomas and meningiomas [22], [23]. Another commonly used descriptor is the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), which models the spatial relationships between voxel intensities. By encoding contrast, homogeneity, and directional texture, GLCM effectively reflects tissue irregularities and intratumoral heterogeneity, properties that have been shown to correlate with tumor behavior and malignancy [24]. Local Binary Patterns (LBP) and histogram-based features also contribute to tumor characterization by capturing microtexture transitions and the global distribution of intensity values, making them robust descriptors for differentiating tumor and non-tumor regions [25], [26]. With the increasing success of DL, many recent studies extract high-level features directly from MRI images using pretrained CNN architectures such as VGG-16, GoogleNet, AlexNet, and ResNet, 

which provide richer and more abstract representations compared to handcrafted features [27], [28], [29], [30]. 

The performance of these pre-processing and feature extraction strategies must be evaluated using standardized metrics to ensure reliable comparison across studies.

## 2) TUMOR CLASSIFICATION WITH DEEP LEARNING 

Khan et al. [39] proposed a hierarchical DL method for brain tumor classification. In this study, the researchers used a Kaggle dataset consisting of MR images of gliomas, meningiomas, pituitary tumors, and no-tumor cases. They applied data resizing, data normalization, and data augmentation and achieved 92.13% classification accuracy. Ghassemi et al. [40] presented a new brain tumor classification model that leveraged pre-training as a discriminator within a generative adversarial network (GAN) to capture key features effectively. Subsequently, the final fully connected layer was 

replaced with a SoftMax classifier to distinguish between three types of tumors. The proposed model comprises six layers and is employed alongside diverse data augmentation techniques. It attains an accuracy of 93.01% on the Figshare dataset. Amou at al. [41] proposed developed a deep CNN architecture for brain tumor classification. This architecture employs a pioneering technique based on Bayesian optimization to optimize the hyperparameters of CNNs. Isunuri and Kakarla [42] proposed a seven-layer CNN to address three types of brain tumor (glioma, meningioma and pituitary) MR image classification. They also optimized computation time through separable convolution. They achieved 97.42% accuracy in their study using Figshare dataset. DL and ML methods is presented. Publications of the existing literature in the last 10 years were included in this study. Following a thorough review, current research gaps were identified, providing directions for future research in this field. When positioned within the broader research landscape, most ML- and DL-based brain tumor classification studies remain at an experimental proof-of-concept stage rather than being clinically deployable systems. Although many models report high accuracy, their real-world applicability is limited by dataset homogeneity, lack of multimodal MRI integration, scarce external validation, and minimal attention to model interpretability. Despite strong results in controlled settings, these models still require rigorous multi-center validation and clinically aligned evaluation before they can be used in real clinical practice. Across the 81 reviewed studies, only a small subset incorporated interpretability analysis, indicating that explainability remains one of the least addressed aspects of current brain tumor classification research. Techniques such as Grad-CAM, SHAP, or counterfactual explanations can increase transparency by revealing which regions or features influence model decisions; however, their usage is inconsistent and often superficial. This lack of explainability further limits the clinical acceptance of current ML and DL systems. 

Across the reviewed studies, several methodological limitations consistently emerge. First, many ML- and DLbased grading and classification models rely on small, single-center, or highly homogeneous datasets, which limits generalizability, especially for meningioma grading where dataset sizes were often below 150 patients. Second, most studies use inconsistent and minimal MRI modalities, frequently depending on only T1ce, T2-FLAIR, or ADC maps, despite the fact that real clinical diagnosis is inherently multimodal; only a few works integrate multiple sequences or assess their complementary value. Third, segmentation quality varies widely, with some studies using fully automatic pipelines (e.g., modified U-Net), while others rely on manual or partially manual tumor delineation—introducing observer bias and making comparisons across studies difficult. Fourth, although DL models often report very high accuracy (96–99%), many depend on simple architectures or transfer learning applied to 2D slices, which may lose important volumetric tumor information; even 3D CNNs often require aggressive preprocessing or augmentation to compensate for data scarcity. Fifth, class imbalance is a persistent issue—especially in grade II–III meningiomas and LGG vs. HGG gliomas—yet a large portion of the studies do not implement systematic imbalance handling (e.g., resampling, cost-sensitive learning), creating inflated accuracy for majority classes. Finally, performance metrics are inconsistently reported across works, and many papers emphasize accuracy while underreporting clinically critical measures such as sensitivity, specificity, or AUC. In clinical settings, evaluation metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, and F1-score play a crucial role in determining the practical utility of AI-based diagnostic systems. High sensitivity is 

essential to minimize false-negative tumor predictions, while specificity is important to reduce false positives that may lead to unnecessary interventions. The F1-score provides a balanced assessment in cases of class imbalance, making it particularly valuable when minority tumor types or grades must be detected reliably. Therefore, these metrics directly shape the clinical decision-making value and safety of ML- and DL-based models. These limitations indicate that systematic imbalance-handling strategies (e.g., resampling or cost-sensitive learning), multimodal fusion, and the adoption of 3D architectures could help mitigate the generalizability issues observed in current studies. Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for larger multimodal datasets, standardized evaluation frameworks, and improved interpretability for ML and DL models to be reliably deployed in clinical practice. 

Studies using Figshare and BraTs datasets are abundant. In addition, the sample sizes used in these experiments are often small. However, it is important to note that frequently used datasets such as Figshare and BraTS have several limitations that could affect the generalizability and clinical relevance of developed models. First, class imbalance is a common issue in these datasets, with certain tumor types or grades significantly underrepresented. Class imbalance often causes models to become biased, achieving good results for majority categories but failing to generalize effectively to underrepresented ones. Additionally, high accuracy values reported in some studies may be misleading in the presence of class imbalance, as accuracy tends to favor the majority class and may not reflect true model performance. Furthermore, most of these datasets are derived from limited sources and lack diversity in terms of imaging protocols, scanner types, and patient demographics. This limitation restricts the ability of models to generalize across different clinical environments. To address these challenges, future studies should prioritize the inclusion of multi-center, multi-device datasets and evaluate models on external data from diverse clinical settings to ensure robust and clinically relevant performance. Additionally, the number of data used in the experiments is small. For this reason, studies with a superior number of data stand out more. 

In recent years, DL methods, especially transfer learning methods, have become increasingly popular in brain tumor type classification, whereas ML methods are less commonly explored. ML techniques, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), generally require smaller datasets and rely on manually extracted features. These approaches are often more interpretable and computationally efficient, making them suitable for studies with limited data or lower computational resources. However, their performance tends to plateau with larger, more complex datasets. In contrast, DL methods, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), automatically learn hierarchical feature representations from raw images and often achieve superior accuracy, especially with large datasets. Nonetheless, DL models typically demand

higher computational resources and are criticized for their ‘‘black-box’’ nature, where the decision-making process is not easily interpretable. In addition to standalone ML and DL approaches, hybrid models that combine handcrafted radiomic features with deep representations have shown promising results in several studies. Such models leverage the interpretability and low-data robustness of ML techniques together with the hierarchical feature extraction capabilities of DL architectures. As reported in multiple works, these hybrid or feature-fusion strategies can improve performance, especially in scenarios with limited datasets, imbalanced classes, or heterogeneous imaging conditions. Therefore, incorporating and further exploring hybrid pipelines represents a valuable future research direction for strengthening tumor classification and grading models. 

To address this limitation, explainable AI (XAI) techniques have gained attention in recent years. Methods such as saliency maps (e.g., Grad-CAM), feature attribution, and counterfactual explanations aim to enhance the transparency of DL models by visualizing the specific image regions or features that influence predictions. These tools help improve the trustworthiness of DL models and support their integration into clinical workflows by enabling healthcare professionals to verify whether the models focus on medically relevant areas. While XAI methods hold promise, they are not yet foolproof; some explanations may lack consistency or reliability. Nevertheless, integrating XAI with DL models represents a significant step toward making brain tumor classification systems more transparent, interpretable, and clinically acceptable. In clinical environments, XAI methods such as Grad-CAM can help clinicians assess whether the model focuses on medically meaningful tumor regions, thereby increasing trust in automated decisions. When the highlighted activation areas align with radiological findings, clinicians are more likely to rely on the predictions, whereas inconsistent or clinically irrelevant heatmaps may signal potential model failure. Thus, XAI tools not only improve transparency but also play a critical role in evaluating the clinical reliability and safety of AI-driven diagnostic systems. 

Glioma has been used more frequently in brain tumor grade classification studies than other tumor types. One of the most important reasons for this is the availability of open data sources. Therefore, it can be said that there is a gap in the literature in studies based on grade predictions of tumors other than glioma. This represents a notable gap, particularly for tumor types with limited publicly available datasets. Moreover, unlike gliomas, many other intracranial tumor types—such as pituitary adenomas, schwannomas, craniopharyngiomas, and medulloblastomas—do not use WHO grade as a primary clinical determinant. Although meningioma grades I–III differ significantly in recurrence risk and therapeutic strategy, yet multi-class grading datasets remain scarce, leading many studies to merge grades or reduce the problem to a binary classification. As a result, grade-based classification studies are naturally concentrated on gliomas and, to a lesser extent, meningiomas, which 

explains their overrepresentation in the literature and the limited availability of grade-labeled datasets for other tumor types. It has been observed that, due to limited data, tumor grades are often merged to simplify the classification task. As a result, multi-class grade classification (e.g., Grade I–II– III) remains an important open research problem for future studies. 

Researchers frequently used ML and DL methods to detect brain tumor type and automatically classify their grades. A total of 81 articles were examined in this review. Each was briefly summarized and its important parameters reported. The use of methods such as transfer learning and ensemble techniques was associated with improved performance. In addition, it is understood from the literature that preprocessing methods are among the critical techniques for classification with MR images. Upon examining the articles, DL methods were found to be particularly successful in MRI classification and showed promise for tumor classification tasks. For instance, several of the reviewed studies reported high classification performance with DL models, often achieving accuracy levels above 95% and F1-scores exceeding 0.90 for tasks such as glioma grade classification or multi-class tumor type prediction. Overall, DL methods have shown strong potential for MRI-based classification and remain promising tools for future tumor classification and grading research.
